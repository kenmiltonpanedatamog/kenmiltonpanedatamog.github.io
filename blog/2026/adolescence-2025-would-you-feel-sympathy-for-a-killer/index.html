Would you feel sympathy for a killer? (Adolescence 2025)

Published on September 11, 2026

2026   ·   media   2026-03-27   ·   essays   archives

Here’s a dilemma: An innocent looking, 13-year-old boy, was accused of killing a girl similar to his age. There was concrete video evidence, but there’s a missing piece—more than the weapon, but the Why?

Motives for crimes are a secondary intel behind the actual evidence—the whys don’t justify the fact. The motive has little or no impact on the verdict to a crime as killing for a reason is not that different to another reason, not unless it is for “self defense” or war, or any other ways we as a society legalized killing another person.

Beyond the dilemma of why, we as the audience, can fall into the trap of the show. They made the accused killer seem innocent, besides, he was just a child—he’s not that scary. We can fall into the trap of attaching hope that this boy will be proven innocent and freed. The show created a facade which can make us sympathize with the accused killer, and I don’t think it’s unreasonable to feel that sympathy especially when watching the first 2 episodes only. The boy pleads he did not do it, and you can see a glimpse in his eyes and tone of voice that he didn’t.

The Season 1 of the show has a limited run of 4 episodes. Episode 1 followed the arrest and initial questioning of the accused. Episode 2 followed the police trying to find answers on the victim and accused killer’s school. Episode 3 followed a psychologist interviewing the boy. And Episode 4 follows the boy’s family months after the chaos.

Episode 1: The chaos

The police raided the Miller family’s house like a terrorist was inside. No showing of search or arrest warrant, just pure force, just to arrest a little boy named Jamie. He was under arrest for suspicion of murder. Obviously, this chaos that ensued brought the boy and his family to feel terror. We followed Jamie on their way to a police facility. What first struck me was how composed he is, he was just 13, the police just arrested him, and he is made to do things that a child must not experience. I won’t spoil much, but I admire how the legal side of this show was at least ethical. I would have hated it if there’s some legal manipulation happening, but even the arresting officers gave respect to the rights of Jamie and his family too. They showed the video evidence in this episode—it showed Jamie following the victim and ultimately stabbing her—he was, as it stands, guilty. But there’s a feeling of not really. Even without the hints of other possibilities, this video still did not convince me that he did it. Jamie’s father, however, did as many viewers are too. But throughout this show, there is a constant choice between seeing Jamie as pure guilty thus evil and seeing and sympathizing with the idea that he was just another victim in this case or that a mental health problem caused him to do what he did. Nonetheless, basing on the facts, he was guilty right then and there. I personally felt sympathy for him, because I wasn’t convinced yet that he did it for some reason. I also wanted to know the Why before passing any judgement. My brain theorized a scenario that he was just set up or if he in fact did it, there’s some mental health issue at play. I know that sympathizing for a criminal is unethical in all ways, but again, he was still accused and therefore was still innocent to me.

Episode 2: The leads

The police continued finding the Why, they haven’t found the weapon and there is no motive. They went to the school that the victim and Jamie went to. They questioned the school and the students, but were met with various red flags. For one, there was a scene where two boys, which I assume are Jamie’s friends, talking about what happened. They mentioned the knife, which immediately raised a red flag, and a hint that Jamie is not a lone actor. My theory that he was set up came from these two boys. Another red flag was the victim’s best friend. The interview act with her just screams that she knows something or she thinks she knows. Later on, she physically abused one of the boys who is a friend of Jamie—she said that he did it—so once again, a hint that it might not be Jamie alone. That same boy even tried to escape when confronted by the police, he was also arrested after admitting that it was his knife which was used. The police did not question the other boy, which is a bit suspicious to me. Finally, one of the police officer’s sons made some confession which I still don’t fully understand. But that confession made his father think of another angle—Jamie is being bullied. The picture is looking clearer: Jamie, if he did do it, was not alone. There is a possibility of the friends setting him up. I just realized I keep saying “setting him up”, I don’t have a concrete definition to this claim but I mean it in a way that Jamie was either provoked to kill her, his friends changed clothes with him to make it look like it’s him, basically conspiracy stuff. Going back to the point, Jamie was bullied by the victim. I think a quote from the show perfectly summarizes what this particular episode is all about; “What you think is more important than what’s true.” Yes, the video evidence exists, but how do we know if there’s a piece of truth that will explain it even better. I wanted to know the full picture rather than the single evidence that Jamie did it.

Episode 3: A masterpiece, a manipulation, and a man confessing

Episode 3 is one of the most brilliant media I’ve ever watched. It is a masterpiece of storytelling, acting, cinematography—and everything about it feels perfect. The actor that played Jamie really did a very good job.

Manipulation is a central theme to this act. It feels like the psychologist is manipulating Jamie, and Jamie also does the same. It felt like a trick sometimes, but I think the whole act was not a trick but indeed a conversation. If there’s someone tricking, it was Jamie as evident to his multiple outbursts when he said something that might prove him guilty. Jamie’s character is just brilliant for his age, and it was evident by his ability to manipulate what he is saying, it reflected in his ability to hide. The last 20 minutes, which is just a rough estimate, is where all of the dots started connecting. Jamie revealed a part of himself he’s been hiding from us—he finally said things that seemed like a confession. Until now, he’s been firm in his stand that he did not do it. But what we heard from him, that he had a knife that night but he did not touch her… that doing so made him better than other men… is a clear admission of the truth. I felt what the psychologist probably felt—I wanted Jamie to be proven good, I wanted him to be free, I wanted him to continue saying that he did not do it—but that didn’t happen.

Episode 4: Goodbye

Our goodbye with Jamie happened at the end of Episode 3. It will be the last time we will ever see him apart from a call he will make in this last episode.

This episode followed the life of Jamie’s family 13 months after the chaos. What really struck me was how normal their lives seemed until they were reminded by the effects of what Jamie did. The first reminder was the vandalism in their van saying “NONCE,” which is an insult apparently that also means pedophile. This started the anger of the father. As they went to a store to buy paint, another reminder appeared. A man approached the father which he recognizes, the man followed him saying that he sympathized and is on Jamie’s side. The father was reminded of what happened, his mind went blank, but when they were about to leave, he saw the kids who vandalised his car. This made his anger into a long outrage which can be felt throughout the act. His wife and daughter stayed silent in his outburst, when suddenly, they received a call from Jamie.

Jamie wanted to let his father know that he is pleading guilty. Silence was heard, then Jamie’s mother and sister spoke. Jamie was not expecting to have his mother and sister on the call, which hurts to think about because it seems like he did not trust them. The call got cut before his father could say the word “don’t”. Emotions started rising, when they got home, it flooded the house. We got to witness a conversation between the parents about Jamie. This conversation seems like a goodbye, an acknowledgement that they need to move on. The father ended the show with such a powerful scene—he went to his son’s room, cried, then tucked a teddy bear as if putting Jamie to sleep.

I was watching that ending at around 2 AM, I was sobbing, I was writing in my diary, I was devastated. I felt no closure from Jamie, I wanted to see him again even if that means seeing him sentenced to prison. I wanted to know what his future will hold despite all that happened.

Conclusion

To answer the headline—Yes, I did sympathize with the accused killer. I grew to like the facade of this innocent boy who hasn’t been proven guilty yet. But honestly, the truth is clear. Jamie killed the girl without any doubt. I think the reason why I still felt sympathy was because I was seeing the hardship that Jamie went through while the death of the girl just merely echoed in the background. The show followed the life of Jamie, so we only got to see his perspective. I believed in his statements that he did not do it, that he did nothing wrong, because the only voice saying otherwise was that video evidence which I still don’t understand why it was not enough evidence in the first place. The show made me want to know why—to know what Jamie thinks rather than the clear truth in front of me. In the end, I got answers to the whys, but I wanted to know what will happen next. The show ended with minimal closure to what happened to Jamie. It ended without looking back at what happened to the girl. But maybe it’s better that we don’t know. We left Jamie finally admitting to his sin, and we can try and think of what happened to him after that—we can try and feel sorry for him—but it doesn’t change the fact that he was guilty. And the real victims here besides the girl and her family, was Jamie’s family—who suffered the pain of losing a child and suffered the guilt that they allowed all of this to happen.
