"I had been educated in the rhythms of the mountain, rhythms in which change was never fundamental, only cyclical. The same sun appeared each morning, swept over the valley and dropped behind the peak. The snows that fell in winter always melted in the spring. Our lives were a cycle—the cycle of the day, the cycle of the seasons—circles of perpetual change that, when complete, meant nothing had changed at all. I believed my family was a part of this immortal pattern, that we were, in some sense, eternal. But eternity belonged only to the mountain."
—TARA WESTOVER

> What if I'm born into a different family? What if I'm born into a different country or century? Would I be the same person?

I asked these question every time I tried to imagine myself in Tara's life. I was never born into an ultra-religious family, nor was born into one which thinks of the worst possible scenarios all the time. I wasn’t born into a time where I cannot go to the medical establishments when I get sick, nor was born to be indoctrinated rather than educated. I was born into a family, a place and time which I now take for granted.

Unlike me, Tara was born into a family whose beliefs left a deep mark into her life. This mark was destructive, fatalistic, and undeserved. Tara lived a life of danger, made more dangerous by the fact that they did not seek medical help when they truly needed it. Tara was raised to believe that the government is against them, that the medical establishments are there to hurt them—set by the government, and that everyone who aren't like them are evil-socialist-whore. Tara was not given the opportunity to be educated normally throughout his childhood and adolescence, where she goes to school with people of similar ages because his father believed that schools are brainwashing institution set up by the government. She was born into this world.

But like me, we shared some similarities, or I thought similar enough to feel like we had the same experiences. Those similar experiences echoed throughout the book.

## The dangers of false beliefs, either by faith or by fear
I could not help myself but assume that the turning point where Tara's father became the way he is, fearful and anxious, was when the Ruby Ridge incident happened. The book opened this topic quickly in the beginning and I cannot stop attributing it to his father's behavior, this is considering how Tara described his father as being a completely different father to his older brothers.

> "The older boys—Tony, Shawn and Tyler—had been raised in a different decade, and it was almost as if they’d had different parents. Their father had never heard of the Weavers
> 
> That history did something to their father."

As a result, the rest of their siblings, including Tara, was raised in isolation, no official birth certificates, no attending school, just them at Buck's Peak. Their father believed that the government is going to target them next, this fear did something to their father. This is a kind of false belief induced by fear.

The other dangerous kind of belief is induced by faith. This kind of faith in something: a God or an idea of something can be seen throughout the book.

Their father believed so much to their faith that he is willingly risking the lives of his children just because he had a false belief against the medical establishments. He thinks that God will cure everything, or he thinks that God has a deterministic plan for their family.

There were many instances in the book where faith won against reason. There were so many things that could have ended badly, or someone could have died. And for what?

## A child indoctrinated is a child deprived of freedom
> What kind of liberty is lost from indoctrination?

Freedom to think for ourselves, this is what Tara did not have the freedom to do, not until entering college.

The book can be summed up to this phrase: A journey from indoctrination to liberation.
